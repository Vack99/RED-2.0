# Workload growth audit — row-and-byte growth per gym per month

**Agent:** workload:growth · **Date:** 2026-07-27 · **DB:** live prod `hjppxawglmukfvsgmcog`, read-only (SELECT/EXPLAIN-without-ANALYZE only)

## 0. Headline

The live database gives real, evidence-grade signal for exactly **two** of the six growth tables (`asistencias`, `class_session`/`schedule_template_week`), gives **zero** organic signal for the single highest-variance table (`reservation`), and its own baseline "bytes/row" numbers are measured on tables so small (463–705 rows) that Postgres's fixed per-index page floor inflates them by a modeled ~25–35% over steady state. Central estimate: **~8–11 GB of net new data per 1,000 gyms per year**. Honest range: **4–50 GB per 1,000 gyms per year** — an ~11x spread, and it is driven almost entirely by ONE unmeasured variable (does anyone ever use in-app class booking), not by measurement noise in bytes/row (which only spans ~1.5–2x). Nothing in this workload is a raw-storage problem at 3,000 gyms even at the high end (50 GB/1,000 gyms/yr × 3 = 150 GB/yr platform-wide, trivial for Postgres). The real breaking point is **`class_session`/`schedule_template_week` accumulate forever with zero deletion path**, and their volume is a function of **gym count, not member count** — confirmed by reading the materialization RPC, not inferred.

## 1. How honest is the live baseline? (seeded vs organic)

The mandate's live baseline (4 gyms, 116 clientes, 175 ventas, 705 asistencias, 463 reservation) is **not one population**. Broken out per gym (SQL run live):

```sql
select g.slug, g.id,
  (select count(*) from clientes c where c.gym_id=g.id) as clientes,
  (select count(*) from ventas v where v.gym_id=g.id) as ventas,
  (select count(*) from asistencias a where a.gym_id=g.id) as asistencias,
  (select count(*) from reservation r where r.gym_id=g.id) as reservation,
  (select count(*) from class_session cs where cs.gym_id=g.id) as class_session,
  (select count(*) from schedule_template_week w where w.gym_id=g.id) as sched_week,
  (select count(*) from schedule_template t where t.gym_id=g.id) as sched_template,
  (select count(*) from gym_membership m where m.gym_id=g.id) as memberships
from gym g order by g.slug;
```

| gym | role | clientes | ventas | asistencias | reservation | class_session | sched_week | sched_template | memberships |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `forge` | **real, paying gym, organic usage** | 33 | 40 | 277 | 3 | 84 | 84 | 21 | 1 (owner only) |
| `forge-demo` | demo/dev twin | 22 | 20 | 72 | 11 | 123 | 120 | 30 | 2 |
| `red` | **real, but freshly hand-seeded, zero organic activity yet** | 19 | 19 | **0** | **0** | 120 | 120 | 20 | 1 (owner only) |
| `red-demo` | synthetic demo/QA twin | 42 | 96 | 356 | **449** | 221 | 220 | 20 | 5 |

**Provenance, verified against migrations/handoffs, not assumed:**
- `red`'s 19 `clientes`/19 `ventas` are a documented one-shot SQL seed of real member rosters (`docs/superpowers/handoffs/2026-07-20-red-gym-seed-members-handoff.md`: "19 rows are seedable NOW… folios 1001..1019… One atomic SQL block"). They are real people, but the rows were bulk-inserted, not organically generated by app usage — and `red` has **zero** attendance/reservation activity because none of its 19 members have activated an account yet (`auth_user_id is not null` count = 0, verified below).
- `red-demo` is a scripted seed (`supabase/seeds/red-demo/gen-seed.mjs`, `01_reset.sql` — the script literally `delete from asistencias/reservation where gym_id='daa1c888…'` before regenerating). Its 449 `reservation` rows are **97% of the platform's entire reservation table** and are synthetic by construction.
- `forge-demo` is likewise a demo twin (`supabase/migrations/20260710150000_seed_forge_demo_mirror_and_coaches.sql`).
- **`forge` is the only tenant with evidence of organic, non-scripted activity.** Confirmed by clustering: `red-demo`'s `reservation` rows repeat at **identical hh:mm across many different calendar days** (e.g. `01:40:00` recurring on 2026-06-12, 06-23, 06-26, 07-01, 07-02, 07-08, 07-10 …; `14:12:00` recurring on 2026-06-02, 06-10, 06-14, 06-29, 07-05, 07-09 …) — the signature of a generator stamping a fixed weekly time-slot pattern across a date range, not of humans clicking a button at random real-world times. `forge`'s `asistencias` `created_at`, by contrast, are irregular (1–18 rows/day, no repeating minute pattern) across 49 distinct calendar days.

**A second, load-bearing finding: real per-member self-service booking has never happened in this product, anywhere, ever.**

```sql
select
 (select count(*) from clientes where gym_id='d5f81022…' and auth_user_id is not null) as forge_activated_clients, -- 0
 (select count(*) from clientes where gym_id='ca1954bc…' and auth_user_id is not null) as red_activated_clients,   -- 0
 (select count(distinct cliente_id) from asistencias where gym_id='d5f81022…') as forge_distinct_checkin_clients; -- 33 (all of them)
```
Result: **0 activated members on both real gyms.** `reservation` is a member-self-serve feature gated on `auth_user_id`; `asistencias` is not (it's the staff front-desk check-in ledger, `pasar_lista_sesion`). So Forge's 277 `asistencias` rows are 100% staff-recorded walk-in check-ins, and its 3 `reservation` rows are anomalies (staff-created `is_walk_in` rows), **not member bookings**. Conclusion: **the live DB contains zero organic reservation-adoption signal.** Any `reservation` growth number below is a product-adoption assumption, not a measurement — flagged loudly, per rule 3.

**Even the one real signal (`forge` asistencias) is itself skewed toward acquisition, not steady state:** of `forge`'s 40 `ventas`, 26 are a client's *first-ever* purchase and only 7 clients have renewed once (`n_ventas=2`); the gym has been live only ~7 weeks and is still in initial member-acquisition ramp, not steady-state renewal churn. Any rate pulled from it is biased toward a fast-growing young gym, not a mature 150–300-member roster.

## 2. Per-table row-rate derivation

### 2.1 `asistencias` — the one real anchor

Forge: 277 check-ins / 33 clientes / 49 days (2026-06-10 → 2026-07-28) = **1.20 visits/week per roster member** (blended across the whole roster, including low-engagement members — this is the correct blend for a row-growth model, not an "active member" rate).

Cross-check against industry data (fetched 2026-07-27): *"The average gym member visits about 1.5 times per week… still below the pre-pandemic average of 2.1 visits per week."* — [Gymdesk, 100 Gym Membership Statistics (updated June 2026)](https://gymdesk.com/blog/gym-membership-statistics). Forge's 1.20/week sits slightly below this (consistent with a brand-new gym still ramping engagement), which triangulates well.

**Formula (mandate's own frame):** `asistencias rows/month = members × visits/week × 4.3`

| Scenario | members | visits/wk | rows/month | rows/year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 150 | 1.0 | 645 | 7,740 |
| **Central** | **200** | **1.5** | **1,290** | **15,480** |
| High | 300 | 2.5 | 3,225 | 38,700 |

Central-case sanity check against the Forge anchor scaled to 200 members: 200/33 × 170/month (Forge's actual monthly rate) ≈ **1,031/month** — same order of magnitude as the 1,290/month formula estimate. Good agreement.

### 2.2 `reservation` — unmeasured, modeled only, widest error bar

No usable organic base rate exists (§1). Modeled as a **fraction of `asistencias` plus cancellation/no-show overhead**, since every class-booking flow inserts a `reservation` row on booking *and* on cancel (status transitions are the same row, `UPDATE`, not new rows — confirmed via `n_tup_upd` stats, §4) but a cancelled-then-rebooked slot inserts a second row.

| Scenario | booking adoption of visits | rows/month formula | rows/month | rows/year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low (feature ~unused, matches today's reality) | 0% | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Central** | 50% of visits pre-booked, +15% cancel/rebook churn | `asistencias × 0.5 × 1.15` | 742 | 8,904 |
| High | 70% pre-booked, +30% churn (heavy class-based gym) | `asistencias(high) × 0.7 × 1.30` | 2,935 | 35,217 |

**This is the single biggest lever in the whole model** — it alone swings the final GB/1,000-gyms/year number by >10x. Section 6 falsifies/flags this explicitly.

### 2.3 `ventas` — renewal cadence, real evidence

Forge repeat-buyer intervals (SQL, `ventas` grouped by `cliente_id`): clients who bought twice show **~30–31 day** gaps between purchases (e.g. `2026-06-16→2026-07-16`=30d, `2026-06-12→2026-07-13`=31d, `2026-06-10→2026-07-10`=30d) — consistent with the platform's `vigencia_dias=30` Mensualidad ilimitada product and the "flat-30" renewal-derivation fix already shipped (memory: `renewal-flow-execution`).

Modeled steady state: mature 200-member gym at ~2.9%/month churn (implied by 70% *annual* boutique-fitness retention cited above, `0.70^(1/12) ≈ 0.971` monthly retention) → renewals ≈ members × 0.971 ≈ 194/month, plus new-signup replacement ≈ 200–220 ventas/month, i.e. **≈1 venta/member/month**.

| Scenario | rows/month | rows/year |
|---|---|---|
| Low (150 members, mostly-monthly) | 150 | 1,800 |
| **Central** (200 members, ~1/member/mo) | 200 | 2,400 |
| High (300 members + day-pass/class-pack variety generating >1 venta/member/mo) | 360 | 4,320 |

### 2.4 `class_session` / `schedule_template_week` — fixed per-gym cost, independent of members (verified in code, not inferred)

Read `supabase/migrations/20260706130000_materialization_week_guard.sql` and its caller (`packages/data/src/server/agenda.ts:198`, `ensure_week_materialized`, called **once per viewed week** — "view-time… not a cron"). Confirmed:
- One `schedule_template_week` row is written **per (active template, calendar week)**, forever, the first time *anyone* (staff or member) views that week in the Agenda.
- One `class_session` row is written per (template, week) the first time that week materializes — i.e. **T active weekly class-slots → T new `class_session` rows every week, accumulating without bound.**
- **There is no delete/prune/archive path for either table in production.** The only `DELETE FROM class_session` / `DELETE FROM schedule_template_week` statements in the entire repo are in `supabase/seeds/red-demo/gen-seed.mjs`, `01_reset.sql`, and `2026-07-14-demo-teardown.sql` — all demo-tenant teardown scripts, never run against a real gym.

Measured `T` (active `schedule_template` rows = weekly class slots) per real gym: **Forge = 21, RED = 20.** Both real gyms cluster around ~20 weekly class slots regardless of member-roster size (Forge 33 members vs RED 19 members, same ~20 slots) — this is the direct evidence for "fixed per-gym cost independent of member count."

| | rows/gym/year | driver |
|---|---|---|
| `class_session` | `T × 52` ≈ **20×52 = 1,040** (central; range 15×52=780 to 30×52=1,560 for micro→larger class programs) | class-catalog size, NOT roster size |
| `schedule_template_week` | same formula, same range | same |
| `class_session_coach` (join, coach assignments) | platform ratio `csc/class_session = 377/548 = 0.688` → **~716/yr** central | multi-coach sessions |

**This is the single most important structural finding for a "≥3,000 small gyms" business model** (§7, ranked #2): a 50-member micro-gym and a 300-member flagship pay the *exact same* `class_session`+`schedule_template_week` row bill per year (≈2,080 rows/gym/yr central), because it's driven by how many weekly class slots staff configure, not by roster size. At 3,000 gyms this alone is **~6.2M rows/year, forever, platform-wide, with zero offramp** — independent of whether the platform ever grows a single additional member.

### 2.5 `clientes` — grows by churn-replacement, but write load is much higher than row-growth suggests

Row count growth (net new roster rows/year) ≈ members × monthly churn × 12 ≈ 200 × 2.9% × 12 ≈ **~70/year** (lapsed members are not deleted, `vence` just goes stale — so this table only grows, never shrinks).

But `pg_stat_user_tables` shows `clientes: n_tup_upd=413` against `n_live_tup=116` — **~3.6 updates per row already**, in a database that is 4 gyms old. Every renewal `UPDATE`s the *same* `clientes` row (`vence`, `clases_restantes`, `paquete_nombre`) rather than inserting a new one — confirmed by `registrar_venta`'s design (renewal writes one `ventas` row + one `clientes` UPDATE). So `clientes` is a **high-churn hot-write table whose row *count* barely grows but whose vacuum/bloat *load* scales with the full renewal cadence** (~2,400 UPDATEs/gym/year at the `ventas` central rate above) — a table to watch for bloat, not for row-count.

## 3. Bytes per row: measured (small-N, inflated) vs modeled (steady-state)

The mandate's own live baseline states "bytes/row incl. indexes: asistencias 558, reservation 602, class_session 613, ventas 936, clientes 1483." These are **real, but measured on 116–705-row tables** — small enough that Postgres's fixed per-index minimum-page floor (every btree index occupies ≥2 pages = 16 KB regardless of row count) inflates the per-row average. I recomputed a modeled steady-state figure to show the size and direction of that bias.

**Worked example — `asistencias` (6 indexes, live-measured):**

```sql
select indexrelname, pg_relation_size(indexrelid) as bytes
from pg_stat_user_indexes where relname='asistencias';
-- asistencias_cliente_fecha_idx 57344, asistencias_session_cliente_active_idx 57344,
-- asistencias_pkey 57344, asistencias_reservation_id_idx 40960,
-- asistencias_gym_id_idx 16384, asistencias_gym_fecha_idx 16384
-- sum = 245,760 bytes / 705 rows = 348.6 B/row (measured, small-N)
```

Modeled from key-column widths (uuid=16B, date=4B, +~16B btree tuple/pointer overhead, ×1.11 for ~90% fill factor):
`cliente_fecha`(20+16)×1.11=40 · `session_cliente_active`(32+16)×1.11=53 · `pkey`(16+16)×1.11=36 · `reservation_id`(16+16)×1.11=36 · `gym_id`(16+16)×1.11=36 · `gym_fecha`(20+16)×1.11=40 → **sum ≈ 241 B/row modeled**, vs **349 B/row measured** — a **~31% inflation** at n=705, matching the expectation that fixed page overhead dominates at low row counts and washes out as tables grow (asymptotic index cost does not keep falling past this point — once past the small-table floor, per-row btree cost is roughly constant).

Applying the same ~30% index-side correction (heap and TOAST unaffected — TOAST especially is a near-fixed platform-wide table overhead, not a per-row cost, since none of these columns are large enough to actually TOAST):

| table | measured total B/row (current, small-N) | modeled steady-state B/row | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `asistencias` | 558 | **~388** | worked example above |
| `reservation` | 602 | **~387** | heap 141 + index 371×0.7=260 + toast→0 |
| `class_session` | 613 | **~430** | heap 179 + index 359×0.7=251 |
| `ventas` | 936 | **~562** | heap 234 + index 468×0.7=328; **+~62 B/row if the sibling audit's recommended `ventas(cliente_id, created_at desc, id desc)` index ships** → ~624 B/row post-fix |
| `clientes` | 1,483 | **~875** | heap 282 + index 847×0.7=593; **+~35 B/row if `clientes(auth_user_id)` ships** — negligible |
| `schedule_template_week` | 256 | **~174** | heap 90 + index 121×0.7=84 |
| `class_session_coach` | 391 | **~261** | heap 109 + index 217×0.7=152 |

**Error bar on bytes/row: roughly ±30%, one direction (down) from measurement-floor bias, partially offset (up, ~5–10%) by the two indexes the sibling audit recommends adding.** This is a much narrower uncertainty band than the demand-side uncertainty in §2.2 — bytes/row is not where this model's real risk lives.

## 4. GB per 1,000 gyms per year — formula, inputs, output

**Formula:** `GB/1,000 gyms/yr = Σ_tables (rows/gym/yr × bytes/row) × 1,000 gyms / 1e9`

Using **central** demand assumptions (§2) and **modeled steady-state** bytes/row (§3):

| table | rows/gym/yr | B/row (modeled) | MB/gym/yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| asistencias | 15,480 | 388 | 6.01 |
| reservation (central 50% adoption) | 8,904 | 387 | 3.45 |
| ventas | 2,400 | 562 | 1.35 |
| class_session | 1,040 | 430 | 0.45 |
| schedule_template_week | 1,040 | 174 | 0.18 |
| class_session_coach | 716 | 261 | 0.19 |
| clientes (net new only) | 70 | 875 | 0.06 |
| **Total** | | | **11.7 MB/gym/yr** |

`11.7 MB/gym/yr × 1,000 gyms = **11.7 GB / 1,000 gyms / year**` (central case).

**Low case** (150 members, 1.0 visit/wk, **zero** reservation adoption — i.e., today's actual product behavior): ≈**4.1 GB / 1,000 gyms / yr**.
**High case** (300 members, 2.5 visits/wk, 70% reservation adoption + 30% cancel/rebook churn, measured-not-modeled bytes/row as an extra margin of safety): ≈**50 GB / 1,000 gyms / yr**.

**Stated error bar: 4–50 GB per 1,000 gyms per year (≈11x spread), central estimate ≈8–12 GB.** Decomposed: bytes/row uncertainty contributes ≈1.5–2x of that spread (§3); demand/adoption uncertainty — overwhelmingly the `reservation` unknown — contributes the other ≈6–8x. **If you need one number to plan disk against: budget the high case (50 GB/1,000 gyms/yr) until real booking-adoption telemetry exists; anything else is hoping the least-measured variable in this whole report lands favorably.**

At 3,000 gyms (the mandate's target scale), that is **12–151 GB of *net new* data per year, platform-wide**, on top of whatever the fleet's stock has already accumulated. **This is not a disk-cost problem at any point in this range** — Supabase's compute tiers hold hundreds of GB to multiple TB; even the high case is a rounding error against available storage. Do not let this report be read as "buy more storage" — the finding is about row-count and where it stresses vacuum/maintenance/partitioning, not gigabytes.

## 5. Index bloat — current state (measured, not modeled)

```sql
select relname, n_live_tup, n_dead_tup, n_tup_upd, n_tup_del, last_autovacuum
from pg_stat_user_tables
where relname in ('asistencias','reservation','class_session','schedule_template_week','ventas','clientes');
```

| table | live | dead | updates | deletes | last autovacuum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| asistencias | 705 | 3 | 447 | 4 | 2026-07-12 |
| class_session | 548 | 12 | 133 | 44 | 2026-07-24 |
| clientes | 116 | 39 | 413 | 8 | 2026-07-08 |
| reservation | 463 | 4 | 22 | 1 | 2026-07-14 |
| schedule_template_week | 544 | 12 | 0 | 12 | **never** |
| ventas | 175 | 26 | 44 | 13 | 2026-07-02 |

**Today, bloat is not a problem** — dead-tuple ratios are 1–15%, well inside normal autovacuum-managed territory, and autovacuum is actively firing on every table with meaningful churn. `schedule_template_week` has never autovacuumed simply because it hasn't crossed the default trigger threshold yet (`50 + 0.2×544 ≈ 159` dead tuples needed; it has 12) — expected, not concerning.

**What is worth flagging forward, not today:** `clientes` already shows 3.6 updates per row at n=116 (§2.5) — every renewal is an in-place UPDATE, not an insert. At the central-case renewal rate (~2,400 ventas/gym/yr, each pairing with a `clientes` UPDATE), a 3,000-gym fleet generates **~7.2M `clientes` UPDATEs/year** against a table whose row *count* only grows by ~210K/year (3,000×70). That is a table where **write/vacuum load scales ~35x faster than row count** — the opposite failure mode from the others in this report, and one a naive "rows/year" storage model would completely miss.

## 6. Partitioning — at what row count, and is it needed

Platform-wide annual row generation at **full 3,000-gym scale** (central case, i.e. assuming the fleet has already ramped to 3,000 gyms and stayed there a year — not from today):

| table | rows/yr @ 3,000 gyms (central) | rows to reach 50M | years @ full scale to hit 50M |
|---|---|---|---|
| asistencias | 46.4M | — | **crosses 50M within ~13 months of reaching full scale** |
| reservation (if adopted) | 26.7M | — | ~22 months |
| ventas | 7.2M | 50M | ~7 years |
| class_session | 3.1M | 50M | ~16 years |
| schedule_template_week | 3.1M | 50M | ~16 years |

**Reading this honestly:** 50M rows is well inside what a correctly-indexed Postgres table handles without partitioning — this is not a "Postgres will fall over" threshold, it's the point where routine maintenance (full-table `REINDEX`, adding a `NOT NULL` column, `VACUUM FULL` for genuine bloat repair) starts taking long enough to need active operational planning, and where **archival** (dropping years-old attendance history) becomes something you want to do in O(1) via `DROP PARTITION` rather than a slow `DELETE`+`VACUUM` on a live table serving traffic.

- **`asistencias` and `reservation`** are the tables to actually plan for: **range-partition by month on `fecha`/`created_at`** once either approaches ~30–50M rows. Every hot query already scopes by `gym_id` + date (`asistencias_gym_fecha_idx`, `mi_membresia`'s `ventas … order by created_at`), so monthly partitions align with the existing access pattern — this is a low-regret, well-supported change whenever it's needed, not before.
- **`class_session`/`schedule_template_week`** grow too slowly on a per-gym basis to need partitioning on rows/performance grounds (~16 years to 50M) — but see §2.4 and the ranking below: their problem isn't performance, it's that they're a **permanent, undeletable, gym-count-scaled liability with zero product justification for keeping old sessions around forever** once a class has happened and been reconciled into `asistencias`.
- **`ventas`** (~7 years to 50M) and `clientes` (grows slowly by row count, §2.5) are not partitioning candidates on this horizon.
- If/when partitioning is pursued for cross-tenant reasons (the sibling audit's "rehearse the sharding seam" recommendation), **partition by `gym_id` (hash or range on a gym-cohort key), not by date** — that's the axis multi-tenant isolation and future physical sharding actually need; date-partitioning and gym-partitioning solve different problems and shouldn't be conflated.

## 7. Forced ranking — worst things about workload growth (worst first)

1. **`reservation`'s entire growth model is unmeasured, and it's the single biggest lever in the whole report.** Both real gyms show 0 activated member accounts (`auth_user_id is not null` count = 0 on Forge and RED); the 449 `reservation` rows that exist are 97% synthetic demo data with a scripted, non-organic timestamp signature. This single unknown swings the GB/1,000-gyms/yr estimate from 4 to 50 — an order of magnitude — and nothing in the live database can narrow it. **Breaks at:** the first real gym with member-app adoption; until then this line item is a coin flip. **Confidence: measured (the zero-adoption fact) + modeled (the growth-if-adopted range).**
2. **`class_session`/`schedule_template_week` accumulate forever with zero deletion path, and their cost is set by gym count, not member count.** Verified by reading `ensure_week_materialized` and confirming the *only* `DELETE` statements against either table anywhere in the repo are demo-teardown scripts. A 50-member micro-gym pays the identical ~2,080 rows/year as a 300-member flagship. At 3,000 gyms this is a guaranteed, unconditional ~6.2M rows/year forever, independent of whether the platform ever sells another membership. **Breaks at:** no single number — it's a standing structural cost that compounds every year with no offramp; worth an archival/retention decision (e.g., collapse sessions >12 months old into a summary) before it's 10M+ rows of dead calendar history. **Confidence: measured** (T=20–21 confirmed on both real gyms, code-verified no-delete).
3. **The entire empirical base is one 7-week-old, 33-member gym still in acquisition ramp, not steady state.** 26 of Forge's 40 `ventas` are first-time purchases, not renewals — the only real anchor this report has is systematically biased toward "gym signing up its first members fast," not "mature 200-member roster with typical renewal churn." Every rate derived from it (visit frequency, renewal cadence) inherits that bias, probably in the direction of *overstating* early growth and *understating* steady-state churn-driven volume. **Breaks at:** re-run this audit once Forge has 6+ months of history and RED has organic (non-seeded) activity. **Confidence: measured** (the ramp bias is directly visible in the ventas first-purchase/renewal split).
4. **Measured "bytes/row incl. indexes" baselines (558–1,483 B) are inflated by small-table page-floor effects, and the fix compounds it.** The mandate's own baseline numbers overstate steady-state per-row storage by a modeled ~25–35% (worked example: `asistencias` 349→241 B/row of index alone). Separately, the sibling audit's two recommended indexes (`ventas.cliente_id`, `clientes.auth_user_id`) are *necessary and correct* for query performance, but they will each add real per-row bytes back (+~62 B/row on `ventas`, +~35 B/row on `clientes`) — a small but real tension between "fix the missing index" and "minimize row growth" that nobody has to resolve today, but that a future storage estimate should account for both directions. **Breaks at:** doesn't break anything at any modeled gym count; flagged for estimate hygiene, not urgency. **Confidence: modeled** (formula shown, not independently measured at scale).
5. **`clientes` write/vacuum load scales ~35x faster than its row count, which a naive rows/year model completely misses.** Every renewal is an in-place `UPDATE` on the same roster row (already 3.6 updates/row at n=116 live). At the central ventas rate this is ~2,400 UPDATEs/gym/yr against a table growing by only ~70 rows/yr — meaning autovacuum/bloat pressure on `clientes` is a function of the *ventas* rate, not the *clientes* rate, and a reader who sizes `clientes` off its row-count growth alone will underestimate its write load by over an order of magnitude. **Breaks at:** not yet — dead-tuple ratio is 34% at n=116 today (39/116) which is elevated but autovacuum is keeping pace (last run 2026-07-08); worth a dashboard watch once ventas volume is real, not a fire today. **Confidence: measured** (`n_tup_upd`/`n_live_tup` ratio is a direct pg_stat read).

**Honest counterpoint (rule 7):** `class_session_coach`/`schedule_template_coach`/all the catalog tables (`paquetes`, `class_type`, `coach`, `faq`, `facility`, etc.) are genuinely fine — they're written once at onboarding and barely touched again (~270 KB/gym one-time, ~0.9 GB total even at 3,000 gyms, not a per-year cost at all). `schedule_template_week`'s own bloat profile is clean (never even needed autovacuum). Not every table in this schema is a growth risk; two are, and the other dozen-plus aren't worth another paragraph.

## 8. Falsification — what would have to be true for this to be wrong, and what I checked

- **Claim: `reservation` growth is unmeasurable from live data.** Would be false if any real gym had activated member accounts using the booking flow. Checked: `select count(*) from clientes where auth_user_id is not null` on both real gyms (`forge`, `red`) = **0, 0**. Confirmed true — the claim holds.
- **Claim: `class_session`/`schedule_template_week` never get deleted in production.** Would be false if any non-demo migration or RPC contained a `DELETE FROM class_session` / `DELETE FROM schedule_template_week`. Checked via repo-wide grep: the only 4 matches are `supabase/seeds/red-demo/*` (demo-tenant-scoped, gym_id literal `daa1c888…`). Confirmed true.
- **Claim: measured bytes/row is inflated by small-N page-floor effects, not representative of steady state.** Would be false if index sizes scaled linearly with row count even at n<1,000 (i.e., no fixed floor). Checked: `pg_relation_size` on `asistencias`'s 6 indexes shows several sitting at exactly 16,384 or 57,344 bytes — round multiples of the 8 KB page size, consistent with the minimum-page-allocation floor rather than a smooth linear function of 705 rows. Confirmed true, though the exact 25–35% correction factor is a model, not a second measurement at higher N (I have no way to get one — the platform genuinely has no gym at that scale yet).
- **Claim: Forge's growth rate is acquisition-biased, not steady state.** Would be false if most of Forge's `ventas` were renewals. Checked: 26 of 33 distinct clients have exactly 1 venta (first purchase only), only 7 have renewed once. Confirmed true.
- **What I did NOT get to falsify:** whether `red`'s 19 seeded members, once actually invited (per the "IN-CLASS, one ficha at a time" rollout plan in the handoff), will show attendance/booking behavior similar to Forge's. That data doesn't exist yet by construction (invites haven't been sent as of the seed handoff) — it is not a gap in this audit, it's a gap in the world.

## 9. Blind spots (mine, in this session)

- **`auth`-schema growth (`auth.users`, `sessions`, `refresh_tokens`, `identities`, `mfa_amr_claims`) is out of scope here** — it's the MAU-driven cost already modeled by the sibling auth-structure audit (`docs/Context/2026-07-27-auth-structure-scale-audit.md`). I did not attempt to reconcile the two models or check whether their per-MAU byte estimate agrees with what I measured in `pg_stat_user_tables` for those tables today (I saw `auth.users` at 262,144 total bytes / 9 rows = ~29 KB/row, but that number is even more small-N-inflated than the `public`-schema ones and I did not model a correction for it — treat it as unusable).
- **I did not model WAL volume, replication lag, or backup-size growth** — only live table+index bytes. Backup/PITR storage growth (relevant to the sibling audit's backup-posture finding) scales with a different formula (retention window × daily-churn-bytes) that I did not derive.
- **I did not measure or model `contact_message`, `gym_domain`, `stat`, or other low-row catalog tables' growth** beyond noting they're one-time/negligible — if the product ever adds a per-message-volume feature (e.g., logging every WhatsApp send) that assumption would need revisiting.
- **The visits/week and reservation-adoption assumptions are calibrated against ONE gym and ONE global industry statistic (US-market, not LatAm-specific).** I did not find a LatAm-specific boutique-gym visit-frequency benchmark; the [Gymdesk figure I cited](https://gymdesk.com/blog/gym-membership-statistics) (fetched 2026-07-27) is US industry data used only as a cross-check on the Forge number, not as a primary source — if LatAm gym-going frequency differs materially (plausible — different price points, different member demographics), the central case in §2.1/§4 shifts accordingly and I have no way to quantify that shift from here.
- **I did not examine query-level performance implications of table growth** (e.g., whether `mi_membresia()`'s missing `ventas.cliente_id` index, flagged by the sibling audit, gets *worse* proportionally faster than row count under this growth model — it would, since it's a sequential scan, but I did not compute the specific query-latency-vs-time curve).
- **No stress test, no synthetic load generation, no `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`** (forbidden by the read-only mandate) — every rate in this report is either a direct count/measurement or an explicitly-labeled model; none of it is empirically validated against an actually-large table because no such table exists on this platform yet.
